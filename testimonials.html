Voices from the fields

Kagiso Motswagole, Maize & Sorghum Farmer — Thamaga, Botswana 15 hectares, farming for 22 years "I have been farming this land since my father handed it to me, and I will be honest — there were years I nearly gave up. Inconsistent rains, army worm, poor soils. But when I started working with an agronomist who introduced me to conservation tillage and proper crop rotation, everything changed. My soil holds moisture longer now, my input costs have come down, and last season I harvested more sorghum per hectare than I ever have in my life. I wish someone had told me about these methods twenty years ago. Farming is hard, but it does not have to be as hard as I was making it."

Boitumelo Sechele, Sunflower Farmer — Mahalapye, Botswana 8 hectares, farming for 9 years "I am a woman farming alone, and people in this area did not always take me seriously. But my fields speak for me now. I started with sunflower because the input costs were manageable and the market was reliable. What made the biggest difference was soil testing — I had been applying fertiliser blindly for years. Once I knew exactly what my soil needed, I stopped wasting money on what it did not. This past season I sold my crop at a good price and used the profit to buy a small grain storage unit. I am building something real here, one season at a time."

Thabo Ramotshabi, Cowpea & Maize Farmer — Francistown, Botswana 30 hectares, farming for 31 years "People think farming is simple — you plant, it rains, you harvest. It is nothing like that. You are a scientist, a businessman, a weather forecaster, and a manual labourer all at once. What has kept me going is staying curious. I attended a field day three years ago where they demonstrated push-pull intercropping for fall armyworm control. I was sceptical at first — I am an old man and I do not change easily. But I tried it on a portion of my land and the difference was remarkable. Far less damage, no chemical sprays on that block at all. Now I have extended it across half my farm. If you stop learning in this business, the land will humble you very quickly."

Digoronto Agric-Shop was founded with a single, clear purpose to make quality agricultural inputs accessible to every farmer in Botswana, whether they till one hectare or one hundred. The name "Digoronto" — meaning fruits in Setswana — reflects our inspiration from the desire to symbolize the ultimate results of a harvest and the hardworking farmers who feed our communities. Starting from our flagship branch in Moshupa, we have grown steadily by listening to farmers, understanding their unique challenges and stocking solutions that actually work in Botswana's climate and soils.
